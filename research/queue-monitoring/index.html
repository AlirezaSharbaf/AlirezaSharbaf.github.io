The timing of information

Suppose the last report showed a short line. The longer that report stays on display, the more opportunities there have been for customers to arrive or leave. An arriving customer uses the report and its age to decide whether joining is worthwhile.

With Krishnamurthy Iyer and Jerry Anunrojwong, I study a provider who chooses when to observe congestion and release a fresh report. The provider’s decision changes who joins, which in turn changes the queue that the next report will describe.

How quickly does a report age?

Consider a two-state illustration in which a server switches between idle and busy. Each spell lasts one time unit on average, with exponentially distributed durations. A fresh observation tells us the state exactly. As the report ages, the probability of a busy server approaches one half.

Comparing information policies

I formulated a dynamic program for choosing when to check the queue, solved the two-state case, and compared four policies across 150 modeled scenarios in Python. The comparisons included no updates, continuous disclosure, timed updates, and optimal signaling.

When checking was free, the best observed gain from timed monitoring over continuous disclosure was 34.9% in modeled throughput. The comparison illustrates how customers’ responses can make the timing of information matter even when observations cost nothing.
