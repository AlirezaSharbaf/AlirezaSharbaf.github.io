Learning from networks of fact-checkers

When several reviewers agree on a claim, how much evidence does that provide? I worked on two versions of this question, one with independent reviewers and one in which reviewers influence one another.

University of Minnesota Twin Cities Information and Decision Systems Lab 2022–2024

Combining independent votes

In the first model, reviewers label statements true or false. Their errors are independent given the truth, but reviewers differ in reliability. We derived the optimal rule for weighting their votes when error rates are known and bounded its probability of error.

In practice, the reviewer error rates may themselves be unknown. We developed an online method that estimates reliability from the votes. In simulation, its decisions agreed with the classifier that knew the error rates on 9,983 of 10,000 statements.

Recovering who influences whom

In the second model, the votes can be dependent. Along a hidden chain, each reviewer either copies the preceding reviewer or reports the true label. We asked which features of that chain could be reconstructed from the votes alone.

Marginal statistics
Order reviewers by how often they vote positive. When one true label is known to be more frequent, those rates identify the direction of the chain.
Pairwise statistics
Use the frequency of disagreements between pairs of reviewers to recover their relative positions.
Sample collection
Look at patterns of split votes across statements. With equally likely true labels, this method and the pairwise method recover the chain up to reversal.

We also derived bounds on how many statements all reviewers must evaluate for reliable recovery. With the other parameters fixed, all three bounds grow logarithmically with the number of reviewers.
